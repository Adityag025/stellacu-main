Inside the 4x organic traffic playbook we ran for a SaaS scale-up.

Field Report SEO Inbound

The brief was straightforward. A B2B SaaS company in workflow automation, $14M ARR, growing 60% year over year on paid alone. Organic traffic had been flat at 22,000 monthly visitors for 18 months. Their CAC had doubled in two years. The CEO's framing: "We have to get off paid. Help us build a real organic engine."

By month 12 of the engagement, organic traffic was at 92,000 monthly visitors. Demos sourced from organic had gone from 4% of pipeline to 31%. Here is what we found, what we changed, and the playbook that produced the result.

The audit revealed three structural problems

We always start an inbound engagement with a five-day audit. In this case, the audit surfaced three issues, none of which were SEO problems in the conventional sense.

The content was written for the wrong reader. Every blog post was written for senior buyers (CTOs, VPs of Engineering). But the actual searcher for their category was the IC or the manager looking for tactical help with the daily problem. The content was speaking to the decision maker, not the searcher.

The architecture was flat. They had 240 blog posts but no topic clusters, no pillar pages, no hub structure. Every post was an island. Google had no signal of category authority because the site offered no map of the category.

Conversion was misaligned. The site had one CTA across every page: "Book a demo." The organic searcher had not yet decided they needed software. Asking for a demo was the wrong ask at the wrong moment in the journey.

The 4 moves that mattered

Move 1: We rebuilt the audience segmentation around the searcher, not the buyer

We mapped 14 jobs-to-be-done across the IC, the manager, and the senior buyer. Then we rewrote the content brief template so every new piece was written for one of those JTBDs with the searcher's seniority in mind, not the decision maker's.

Move 2: We built four pillar hubs and migrated existing content under them

We took the 240 existing posts, mapped them to four pillar topics, and rebuilt the information architecture so every post sat under a hub. Within six months, three of the four pillar hubs were ranking in the top 5 for their target queries. Two were in position 1.

Move 3: We changed the CTA model

Instead of "Book a demo," we built three tiered offers: IC-level templates (no email gate), manager-level benchmarking reports (email gate), and senior-buyer-level demos (high-intent gate). Email captures went up 9x. Demo requests from organic went up 3.2x.

Move 4: We made publishing operational, not project-based

We replaced the content calendar with a publishing system (three pieces per pillar, every two weeks, on a 90-day cycle). Production velocity went up. Quality went up. The team stopped arguing in editorial meetings about what to write next.

The results in 12 months

  • Organic traffic: 22,000 to 92,000 monthly visitors (4.2x)
  • Organic-sourced demos: 4% to 31% of total pipeline
  • Cost per organic-sourced opportunity: $0 marginal vs. $1,800 paid
  • Paid spend reduced 22% without revenue impact
  • Pipeline mix shifted from 78% paid / 22% organic to 51% paid / 49% organic

What this engagement taught us about inbound

One. Most inbound engines aren't broken at the SEO level. They're broken at the architecture level. Tactics applied to bad architecture don't compound. Architecture applied to mediocre tactics does.

Two. Match the offer to the searcher's stage, not the company's stage. Asking a stage-one searcher for a demo is the inbound equivalent of asking someone for marriage on a first date.

Three. Publish on a system, not a schedule. Schedules drift. Systems compound. A 90-day publishing system that ships consistently will beat a sporadic high-quality calendar every time.

If your organic engine has flatlined and paid has crept past 70% of acquisition spend, the diagnosis matters more than the next tactic.
