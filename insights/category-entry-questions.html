The 5 questions we ask before any category entry.

Diagnostic Strategy Business Advisory

Every quarter a founder walks into our office and tells us they're entering a new category. Most of the time, they're not. They're re-labeling an existing one, which is a different problem with a different solution. Telling the difference is the first job.

Before we sign off on any category-entry engagement, we run the same five questions. If a client can't answer four of them clearly, we don't recommend the entry. We recommend a clarity engagement first. Here are the questions, what we're listening for, and what the answer usually reveals.

Question 1. Is the category real, or are you re-labeling an existing one?

Most "new category" claims are repositioning claims in costume. There's nothing wrong with repositioning. It's faster, cheaper, and converts better than category creation. But the strategies look nothing alike, so if you confuse the two, you'll spend category-creation money on a repositioning problem.

The test: can you name three competitors who would lose money if your category took off? If yes, it's a positioning move (you're stealing share). If no, it's a category move (you're growing the pie). The first needs sharper messaging. The second needs a five-year horizon and a much bigger budget.

Question 2. Who is already defending this space, and what would they say about you?

Every market has incumbents who benefit from the existing frame. Before you launch, write the press release the incumbent would issue in response to yours. If you can write it in three paragraphs that sound damaging, your category claim is fragile. Strengthen it.

If you can't write the press release at all, the incumbent doesn't see you as a threat yet. Which is sometimes a good thing and sometimes the worst thing.

Question 3. Where will the first 100 customers come from?

Specifically. Not "mid-market SaaS in North America." That's a deck answer. We want names, communities, conferences, partners, content surfaces. If the answer is a list, the category entry has a path. If the answer is "from search and content," you're either six quarters from your first 100 customers, or you don't have a category, just a product.

This question kills more category-entry strategies than the other four combined.

Question 4. What can't you say in 18 months?

A category needs constraints. If you can sell to anyone, in any vertical, with any use case, you've described a feature, not a category. Force yourself to name three things you won't claim. "We won't serve regulated industries until 2027." "We won't compete on price, ever." "We won't ship a self-serve tier."

Constraints make the category legible. Categories without constraints sound like brochures.

Question 5. Who has to change behavior for this to work?

Real categories ask someone to change something. A buyer to budget differently. An end user to learn a new workflow. A market to accept a new metric. If your category doesn't ask for any behavior change, you're not creating a category. You're contributing to an existing one without permission.

This is the hardest of the five. Founders resist it because behavior change sounds like adoption risk, and they've been trained to minimize that. But the brands that have created categories in the last decade (Stripe, Notion, Figma, Snowflake) all asked for specific behavior changes and made the case relentlessly. If you're not willing to make the case, the category isn't ready to be entered.

How we score it

We don't score the answers. We score the confidence of the answers.

  • Five clear, specific answers means the category is real and you should enter it
  • Three to four clear answers means there's a real category claim hiding inside a positioning move, and we run a clarity engagement first
  • Fewer than three means the founder is using "category" as a synonym for "I'm excited about my product." Which is fine, but it's not a strategy
