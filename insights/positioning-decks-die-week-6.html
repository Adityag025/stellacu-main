Why most positioning decks die in week 6 (and what to ship instead).

POV Positioning Business Advisory

You've seen this happen. A positioning project ends. The deck gets shared. Everyone says it's great. Six weeks later, the sales team is still pitching the old positioning, the website hasn't changed, the CMO is in meetings about something else, and the agency that wrote the deck is on a different account.

The deck didn't die. It was never alive. Most positioning decks aren't artifacts. They're performances. They prove the positioning consultant did the work. They don't help anyone do the work afterward.

The structural problem with decks

A 60-slide positioning deck is a presentation format. It exists to be shown, once, to a room. It is not a working document. It is not a writing source. It is not a sales asset. It cannot be searched, linked, or pulled from.

But the company needs to do all four of those things, every day, for years. So the deck gets archived. The team moves on. The positioning never operationalizes.

Six weeks later, when someone asks "what is our positioning again," nobody opens the deck. They guess. And the new guess becomes the unofficial positioning.

What we ship instead

We don't ship decks. We ship a messaging architecture, which is a different format with different physics. It is a single document, usually 8 to 12 pages, written in plain English, structured like reference documentation. It contains:

  • The category positioning. One paragraph. The frame inside which everything else makes sense.
  • The three product narratives. Each one written as a paragraph a salesperson could read aloud on a call.
  • The segment-specific value propositions. One per ICP segment, mapped to the buyer's actual job-to-be-done.
  • The proof points. Specific numbers, names, customer outcomes. Footnoted. Updatable.
  • The objection responses. What customers actually say. What we actually answer.
  • The "won't say" list. What we are deliberately not claiming, and why.

Then we run a 90-minute workshop with sales, marketing, product, and CS to walk through it. Then we put it in a single living document everyone has access to. Then we revisit it every 90 days.

Six months later, when someone asks "what is our positioning again," they open the document. They find the answer. They quote it accurately. The positioning operationalizes because the format was built to be operational.

The test we use

There's a single test that tells you whether a positioning artifact will survive past week 6. Show it to someone outside the room who wasn't in the original meeting. Ask them to use it to write a cold email. If they can, you've shipped architecture. If they can't, you've shipped theater.

Why decks keep winning anyway

Decks survive because they're easy to present and easy to approve. A 60-slide deck looks like work. A 10-page document looks like a memo, and memos don't impress boards. Which is the actual problem, and the reason we still write the deck at the end. We use it for the board meeting. We use the messaging architecture for the daily work.

The deck is the trophy. The document is the engine. If you only ship the trophy, you've spent positioning money on a positioning artifact nobody uses. Which is fine, but it isn't growth.
